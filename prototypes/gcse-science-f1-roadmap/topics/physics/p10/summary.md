# Current, potential difference and resistance

Current is the rate at which charge passes a point:

`I = Q / t`

Current `I` is measured in amperes (`A`), charge `Q` in coulombs (`C`), and time `t` in seconds (`s`).
Charge is transferred around a complete circuit; current is not consumed by a component.

Potential difference is the energy transferred per unit charge between two points.
It is measured in volts (`V`); it does not flow around a circuit.
For a component obeying Ohm's law at constant conditions:

`V = IR`

Resistance `R` is measured in ohms (`ohm`).
Rearrange before substituting, use compatible units, and check that the result has the requested unit.
Resistance is not fixed for every component: changing conditions can produce a non-linear current-voltage characteristic.

On a current-against-potential-difference graph, potential difference is on the horizontal axis and current is on the vertical axis.
A straight line through the origin represents constant resistance; a curve represents changing resistance.
If the axes are reversed, the meaning of the gradient must also be reversed.

Circuit symbols are agreed representations of components and connections, not pictures of their physical appearance.
Use them consistently when tracing a complete path and deciding where current or potential difference is measured.

High-voltage warning information on a hybrid system tells trained marshals how to interpret whether damaged equipment may be unsafe to touch.
It is not an invitation to inspect or work on a high-voltage system.
