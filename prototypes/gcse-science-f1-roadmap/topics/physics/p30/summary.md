# Half-life, contamination and nuclear medicine

The **half-life** of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei, or its activity, to fall to half its previous value.
After `n` half-lives, the remaining activity is the initial activity divided by `2^n`.
This is repeated halving, not subtraction of one fixed amount.

A detector may record background radiation as well as radiation from a sample.
When a question provides a background count, subtract it before applying half-life to the source count and add it back only if the required answer is the detector's total reading.
Activity and remaining mass are related to how much radioactive material remains, but they are not the same quantity.

For this GCSE distinction, **irradiation** means exposure to ionising radiation, while **contamination** means unwanted radioactive material is on or inside an object or person.
Exposure alone does not imply that radioactive material has been transferred or that the irradiated object remains radioactive.
This wording is a conservative curriculum distinction; the selected pages support external and internal exposure concepts but do not present this exact GCSE terminology as a matched definition.

Nuclear medicine can use short-lived radioactive tracers for diagnosis and radioactive sources for treatment.
Evaluation balances useful information or treatment against radiation dose and other risks, and must remain analytical rather than recommending exposure.
A sealed smoke-alarm source illustrates the distinction: the casing helps prevent radioactive material escaping, while emitted radiation ionises air inside the detector.
