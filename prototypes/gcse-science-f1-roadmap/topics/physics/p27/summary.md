# Stars and stellar life cycles

A star begins when gravity pulls gas and dust in a nebula together.
As the collapsing material forms a protostar, its temperature rises.
When the core becomes hot and dense enough, nuclear fusion begins and the star enters its main-sequence stage.

Fusion joins light nuclei and transfers energy; it is a nuclear process, not chemical burning.
During the main sequence, inward gravitational collapse is balanced by effects associated with the hot, high-pressure interior.

A star's later evolution depends strongly on its initial mass.
A star with a mass similar to the Sun expands into a red giant, sheds outer material and leaves a white dwarf.
A more massive star can become a red supergiant, explode as a supernova and leave a neutron star or, for the most massive remnants, a black hole.
Not every star follows one identical path.

Stellar processes at different stages and events formed elements before the Solar System existed.
This is the origin context for carbon, aluminium and iron in a racing car and for iron atoms in steel tools, but it does not mean that every element forms in one identical stellar stage.
