# Infrared radiation and thermal surfaces

All objects above absolute zero emit and absorb thermal radiation, much of which is infrared at everyday temperatures.
For the school comparison at the same temperature and area, a dull black surface is a better emitter and absorber than a shiny metal surface.
A shiny reflective surface is a poorer emitter and absorber in that comparison.

Surface finish therefore affects cooling by infrared emission.
For four otherwise identical hot cans, the dull black can is predicted to cool fastest if infrared emission is the relevant changed factor.
A fair comparison keeps exposed area, liquid amount, starting temperature, surroundings, measurement times, and other can features the same.
Repeated temperature readings can be compared on cooling curves; a faster initial temperature fall gives a steeper downward gradient.

A thermal camera detects infrared radiation rather than measuring temperature directly.
Its displayed temperature depends on surface emissivity and reflected radiation, so equal-temperature surfaces can appear different and shiny surfaces can give misleading readings.
This qualifies interpretation of thermal images of tyres, brakes, and differently finished components.
The hot-can context is for prediction and data interpretation only, not unsupervised handling of hot apparatus.
