# Ionising radiation and penetration

Unstable nuclei can emit **alpha**, **beta** or **gamma** radiation.
An alpha particle contains two protons and two neutrons, beta radiation consists of fast electrons emitted from a nucleus, and gamma radiation is electromagnetic radiation.

Alpha is strongly ionising but travels only a short distance and is stopped by paper or skin.
Beta is less ionising and more penetrating, and thin aluminium can stop it.
Gamma is weakly ionising compared with alpha but highly penetrating, so reducing external gamma exposure requires thick shielding such as lead or concrete.
Penetrating power and ionising ability are not the same property.

Radioactive decay is random for one unstable nucleus, but a large sample has a predictable **half-life**: the time for its number of undecayed nuclei, or its activity, to fall to half its previous value.
The amount halves repeatedly rather than falling by a fixed amount each half-life.

Ionising radiation can damage living cells, so a use must be weighed against exposure and controlled by trained professionals.
Examples include sealed alpha sources in smoke alarms and medical X-rays.
X-rays are electromagnetic radiation produced differently from alpha, beta and gamma emissions, so an X-ray or CT inspection must not be described as alpha, beta or gamma decay.
Being irradiated does not by itself mean that an object remains radioactive.
